## Introduction

Dynasty Nova finally separates interplanetary missiles from regular defenses: they leave the order of battle and become a weapon in their own right, with their own silo, their own strike and their own interception rules.

## Missiles leave the order of battle

### The bug that had to be fixed

Until now, interceptors and long-range warheads were set up in the game data like ordinary defenses, with placeholder shield and attack values. The result: every stored warhead joined the defender's order of battle and dealt its 12,000 attack points for free, in every round of every defensive battle. Yet a stored missile isn't supposed to fight on its own.

### A silo, not a battlefield

Missiles now have a status of their own, separate from defenses: they never join an order of battle, never take losses and never leave debris. Their capacity depends on a dedicated silo: 10 slots per level, with one slot per interceptor and two per warhead, counting both the missiles already in stock and those still on order. Stored missiles still count toward the player's points, as in OGame: firing them removes those points right away.

## Launching a strike: a one-way trip

### Range and flight time set by the server alone

A strike is launched as a mission in its own right, with a range capped at five times the Impulse Drive level minus one system, never across an entire galaxy. Range, distance and flight time come from the server alone: no movement rule is replayed in the interface, so the game never shows a wrong flight time for a decision that can't be undone.

### Two-step confirmation, no recall

Launching a strike takes a two-step confirmation, because once the warheads are gone, they never come back, even if the target planet is abandoned in the meantime. The salvo shows up on the same route card as fleet movements, but with no recall button and no return leg: a missile can't be called back.

## What happens on impact

### One interceptor destroys one warhead, no dice roll

Against an incoming strike, an interceptor destroys exactly one enemy warhead and is used up in the process: no dice roll, no probability, just a straight trade. The warheads that get through form a pool of damage, proportional to their power and to the attacker's weapons technology, which is spent on the defense grid, starting with the toughest defense or with the target the attacker designated.

### Damage that doesn't repair itself

A defense destroyed by a missile strike never repairs automatically, unlike damage from a regular battle. That's a deliberate choice: it's precisely what makes investing in missiles more worthwhile than sending an entire fleet for the same result. Missiles also never target a building, a ship in orbit or the resource stockpile: only defenses.

## Conclusion

Interplanetary missiles become a harassment weapon in their own right: quick to launch, impossible to recall, and able to inflict damage that no automatic repair will ever erase. Enough to rethink a planet's defense beyond fleet math alone.
