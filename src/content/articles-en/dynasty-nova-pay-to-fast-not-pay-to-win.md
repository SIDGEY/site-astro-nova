## Introduction

In a strategy game where empires clash indirectly, the shop is the screen that draws the most suspicion. That's why it has to prove, at first glance, a simple rule we follow at Dynasty Nova: you pay to speed up your progress, never to get an advantage other players have no way of catching up with.

## Why pay-to-win breaks strategy

### An advantage nobody else can catch up with

Pay-to-win means selling power that free players can't reach, no matter how much time they put in. In a game where strategy rests on choices that genuinely shape your progress, not on a simple click, that kind of imbalance cancels out the very point of competing with other empires.

### Pay-to-fast: the same result, just faster

Pay-to-fast follows a different rule: anything you can buy, a free player can also get by playing longer. Real money buys time, never a ceiling the game alone couldn't get you past.

## How our shop proves it

### One currency, never a hidden shortcut

Dynasty Nova's current shop has just two tabs: **Credits**, for packs of Stellar Points bought with real money, and **Avatars**, for cosmetic customization. This simplicity isn't only about usability: it makes it structurally visible that there is only one premium currency, and that it never unlocks an advantage hidden somewhere else.

### A free option on the same level as the paid packs

Next to the paid packs, a **rewarded ad** card sits in the same grid, at the same size: it's not a second-best option tucked away at the bottom of the page, it's a real alternative for any player who doesn't want to spend a cent.

## Cosmetics, monetization without the risk

Customizable avatars point to a third way: purely aesthetic monetization, organized by rarity tiers, that doesn't touch any game mechanic. It's the kind of purchase that never calls the fairness of a game into question, since it changes nothing about a fleet's strength or how fast research progresses.

## Conclusion

A “pay-to-fast, never pay-to-win” charter written into a game description convinces no one if the interface tells a different story. Trust is built in the design of the shop itself: what it puts forward, what stays free, and what real money can never buy.

At Dynasty Nova, monetization isn't just a matter of economic rules: it's a matter of design. A shop that clearly shows its free option, keeps time separate from power and confines real money to cosmetics builds a kind of trust that no charter can replace.
